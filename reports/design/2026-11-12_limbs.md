# Limbs: what the model grows, what real birch has, and a limb rule

## In short

1. **By tape and scales the model's limbs are too thick, not too thin.** In a stand its thickest living limb is 7.0 cm at 50 and 8.3 cm at 60. On Finnish stand birch of the same stem size, though older and slower-grown, the thickest living branch is about 4 cm. Four sets of birch measurements, by hand or by weight, point the same way.
2. **Only the laser scans said "too thin", and they can't carry that.** The software that turns scans into branches reads wood under 2.5 cm about 73 % too thick (tested on two ash trees). Read with that in mind, the scans don't show the model's limbs to be thin.
3. **The model's limbs are many, even and short-lived:** 65 at 30, 51 at 50, 45 at 60. The largest carries 7-13 % of the leaves. A limb stops lengthening at 8 years, has most leaf at 12 and dies at 19. The tuned rate changes none of that. It changes only how thick they are.
4. **The rule designed here** lets a limb thicken in step with the light on its own leaves. With today's rate it brings branch wood to about 1.0 / 1.2 of the Finnish figure at 50 / 60 (now 1.47 / 1.76). At my desk it does no better than simply lowering the rate to 0.15-0.20. So it is offered as a choice, not as a need.
5. **"Fewer and thicker" fits a birch grown in the open, where nothing is measured yet,** in the model or in real trees. The first step there is to count the open-grown model's limbs (a logging run), not to change a rule. **Forks are real in stands too:** a third of trees by 40, where the model has a tenth at most.

- A tree makes 143 (3) limbs in 60 years; 97 have died by 60. Life: 19.3 years (10th-90th percentile 15-28; longest 34). Every limb started before year 21 is dead at 60. The oldest living limb at 60 is 35 (2) years old (39 in one tree).
- **What ends it:** in 98 % of deaths the limb's own carbon had fallen under its upkeep in its last year (the carbon test). The light on its leaves was then a third of the best-lit limbs' (median 0.33; 93 % under 0.5). 22 % die at or under the crown base, the rest inside the lower crown.
- **A limb's course:** its axis last lengthens at 8 years (90th percentile 18), its leaf peaks at 12 (1.3 m², at two thirds of its life) and it dies with 0.2 m². Its ring peaks at 8-10 years (0.8-0.9 mm of radius) and is 0.07 mm in its last three.
- **Rank doesn't hold when young.** Of the five leafiest limbs at 30, 1.7 are among the five thickest at 40 (3.2 from 40 to 50; 3.8 from 50 to 60).
- **Low limbs live too long.** Limbs on the lowest 3 m live 17 years and the last dies at year 28. At 20 years 16 are alive, at 25 years 10 (lowest live branch 0.38 and 0.89 m).

**Read together.** Real stand birch has thin branches at about 42°, thickest near 3 cm when young and 4 cm when old; they stop making rings and die from below; a quarter to a third of trees fork. Four sets of birch measurements say the model's limbs are too thick: Repola's weights, and Heräjärvi's, Niemistö's and Stener's knots. Two general rules say the same: the area sum, and branches being mostly sapwood. With the rate off the thickest limb (4.1 / 4.4 cm at 50 / 60) matches Heräjärvi, but branch wood is then 0.47 / 0.44 of Repola. So no rate meets both Repola's weights and Heräjärvi's knots; rates of 0.10-0.20 sit between them.

## 4. The design

**Part R, the one rule: a limb's ring goes with the light on its own leaves.** Real branches thicken where they are lit and stop where they are shaded. Today every limb replaces the same share of its sapwood.

- Today, on each living internode *i* of a side axis: kept_i = (1 − λ_i) x S_i and ring_i = max(H x A_i − kept_i, 0), with λ_i = s x min(r_t / r_i, 1). S_i is last year's sapwood (m²), A_i the leaf area beyond it (m²), r_i its radius (m).
- With R: **λ_i = s x min(r_t / r_i, 1) x q_L.** q_L (0 to 1) is the leaf-weighted mean light on the buds of the limb the internode sits on, as the shedding test reads it each spring (`growth.py:843`; 1 = open sky).

| Value | Now | From |
|---|---|---|
| H, sapwood per leaf area | 1.24e-4 m² per m² | The species file's label (current-year shoots); not rechecked |
| s, share of a lit limb's sapwood replaced a year | 0.30 | TUNED against Repola's Eq. 9 in test A. Kept. After a run it would be retuned against A3 at 50 and 60, or left |
| r_t | 0.02 m | TUNED against branch wood in the open. Kept |
| q_L | computed | No new number. The straight-line form is borrowed from maple (Sone 2005, abstract); for birch only the direction is read (Mäkinen 2002, abstract) |

- **Switch:** `pipe_model.branch_ring_by_light`, off by default. It changes one function, `_sapwood_loss` (`growth.py:2516-2528`; called at `1802` and `2930`). The compiled ring code takes the kept sapwood as an input, so it does not change. No new node; one multiplication a node a year.
- **Replaces:** one rate for every limb. It does not replace the tuned 0.30, which becomes the rate of a fully lit limb.
- **Cost elsewhere (SUSPECTED, from the 0.15 arm, whose wood cut is nearest):** it frees about a third of branch ring carbon, which goes to stem and leaf. Stand trees: stem +2.3 / +2.8 cm at 50 / 60, basal area a tree +18 / +19 %, leaf area index +0.14 / +0.31, light −0.0 / −1.3 points, crown ratio +0.03 at 60, stem wood over Eq. 7 0.93 / 1.02, the leader's height unchanged, the lowest live branch 0.4 / 0.7 m lower. Forests: the basal-area guard (28.5) has 6 % of room at 60 and the Baltic leaf row none at 50. So a forest fail is as likely as for a rate of 0.15.

**Forks: too few and too late, but no rule yet.** The model reads 0 of 20 at 10 and 1-2 of 20 at 40 against a measured 6-12 % and 35 %. Today a second top can start only after year 30, once the leader has slowed (first at year 35, SD 2), and a lost leader is replaced by one shoot, never two. A rule would let two shoots take over after a lost leader, and a second top start when the source saw them. I design neither: the sources define neither "double leader" nor "double top", so the owner must first say which reading counts (section 7).

**The open-grown tree: count first.** It has no limb record at all, so no rule is designed for it here.

- The number of limbs, their length, their life and how leaf is shared do not change: 65 / 51 / 45 limbs, 4.0-4.2 m, 19 years, 7 / 9 / 13 %.
- **All parts together** is R alone. The counts change no growth.

**The falsifying test, inside one 20-tree run with the limb log.** If the mechanism is right, all four must hold; any one failing means the wood is not where the rule assumes.
1. Of the wood taken off, at least two thirds comes off limbs older than 20 years, at 50 and at 60 (desk: 80 / 94 %).
2. At equal length, the upper-half limb's base is at least 0.75 of the lower-half limb's at 50 and at 60 (desk: 0.82 / 0.83; today 0.65 / 0.67).
3. Limbs over 5 cm fall by more than half at 50 and at 60 while limbs over 2 cm fall by under 10 % (desk: −78 / −62 % and −2 / −1 %).
4. Limb count, 90th-percentile length and median life stay within 2 paired SE of the control. If they move, something other than the ring changed.
- And one comparison that decides whether R is worth keeping: against the flat 0.15 and 0.20 arms on the same seeds, R must be nearer the band's middle at both ages or leave fewer limbs over 5 cm by more than 2 paired SE. If not, the flat rate is the simpler answer.

## 6. The targets and the scans, honestly

- **No target is changed here,** and none is proposed. The A3 band (0.76-1.31) stays; the row stays "tuned against".
- **The scans can test:** that a birch of 15-20 m has limbs at all heights, their angle (58 / 48 / 68° low / mid / top), and roughly how many limbs are over 5 cm (3; 1-26), where the software's error is smaller. **They cannot test:** anything under about 2.5 cm, so not "limbs over 2 cm", not branch length at 1 cm, and not limb over stem when the limb is thin. Six trees, one country, one software, no felled check, sizes of ages 30-40 only: nothing at 50 or 60.
- **Step B's rule** "limbs not further from the Nordic scans by more than 2 paired SE" sent every rate to the owner. It rests on the three measures the scans cannot test. That is for the owner to keep or drop; I change nothing.
- **Repola's A3** is weights of 127 felled trees from 24 stands, up to 38 cm, bark included, a third of them downy birch. **Heräjärvi's knots** are older, slower trees than the model's, and stop where the stem is 12 cm; a fast-grown thinned tree may carry thicker branches (Niemistö: thicker with faster growth). So "about 4 cm" is a floor for the model's tree, not a target (SUSPECTED 4-6 cm).
- **Candidates of more measured data, for the owner:** (1) Mäkinen, Ojansuu & Niemistö 2003, the branch models for planted silver birch (paywall). (2) Ilomäki et al. 2003, the slope of branch area on stem area (paywall). (3) Sellin & Kupper 2006, sapwood per leaf area by crown position (paywall). (4) The full texts of Mäkinen 2002 and Niemistö 1995a (paywall). (5) Kärkkäinen 1976, branch bark share (a 14.8 MB download at the publisher). (6) Heräjärvi's 2002 thesis at Luke's repository, which may hold the knot figures as tables. (7) Van Den Berge et al. 2021, eleven hedgerow birches as cylinder models (CC BY 4.0): the only reference for open-grown limbs over 5 cm, with the scans' limits. (8) The big limbs of the six reference photos, counted with the stem-trace method on the PC.

## 7. The smallest version worth building first, and how it would be judged

3. **Judged on stand trees first** (20 seeds, paired with `node_t2_kt`, limb log on): the plan's rules for B (A3 inside 0.76-1.31 at 50 and 60; carbon turned down at most 5 %; leaf area index 3.6-5.4; light at most 19.8; the curve holds the leader), the four tests of section 5, and the comparison with the flat arms.
